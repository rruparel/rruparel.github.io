Read Your Way

Five people with different connections to type 2 diabetes read the same two pages of a diabetes guide while thinking out loud. Their histories, relationships and trusted sources shaped what they noticed, skipped and took away.

What the study found

People read on behalf of someone else

The guide speaks to “you,” the person with diabetes. Yet only one of the five participants was reading for herself. The other four read it for a parent or another family member, and the person they pictured shaped what they paid attention to.

“He’s not gonna read this. He would hand this to me.”P3, caring for her father

Medical words can build trust but can also wear it down

How a medical word landed depended on the reader’s life. For the two participants who work in health, terms like “hypoglycemia” were a bridge to their doctors. Years of caregiving had made the same words familiar to P3. For P4, they were “doctor words” to skip. When the page fell short, people turned to sources they already trusted: P4 to her daughter, P3 to her dad’s pharmacist, and P5 to AI tools like Gemini.

“So it’s kind of become my, like, translator.”P5, on using AI to make sense of his dad’s lab reports

One line can change who gets blamed

Reading that stress or illness can raise blood sugar led one participant to rethink what “poorly controlled” means, for herself and for a friend.

“Now I think, maybe sometimes you did your part and still it went up.”P4, by the end of her interview

Read it the way they did

Start with the original page, then turn on a participant to see what reading it was like for them: words that turned into noise, “you” that pointed at the wrong person, answers that weren’t there. Every effect comes from something they said, and their own words appear in handwriting.

The handout is condensed from pages 9 and 10 of the ADA guide. The effects are interpretations of what participants described, not how the guide actually looks.

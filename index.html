About Roma

My work sits where healthcare, technology, and the people who use them come together.

I'm passionate about health, and each step of my career has shaped how I approach it. Hands-on clinical work at Kaiser Permanente showed me the impact of public health and preventive care, which led me to an MPH in community health at USC. Augmedix introduced me to health tech, and Stryker Neurovascular taught me the regulatory side of medical technology. Together, these experiences brought me to Google, where I can help improve health and well-being at scale.

There, I lead mixed-methods research for health AI. That means listening closely to the people these tools are meant for, and turning what I learn into product strategy and design.

What drives me is building technology that helps close health gaps and improves health for whole communities.

I recently started my Doctor of Public Health (DrPH) at UC Berkeley, where I'm building the leadership and research skills to design equitable health programs at a national or global scale. My doctoral research explores how AI tools can help people with limited access to healthcare get the care they need.

Case study

Bringing AMIE into the healthcare triad

How patients, clinicians, and the physicians supervising an AI experienced a diagnostic AI in a real clinic.

Setting
Primary care clinic, Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC)
My interviews
20 patients, 10 clinicians, 5 AI supervisors
Collaborator
Ava Homiar, BIDMC

Background

AMIE (Articulate Medical Intelligence Explorer) is a research AI system from Google for diagnostic conversations. In a prospective feasibility study at a BIDMC primary care clinic, patients chatted with AMIE about their symptoms before their urgent care appointments, while physicians supervised every conversation in real time.

The question

How did patients, clinicians, and AI supervisors experience talking to AMIE? What worked well, and where were the opportunities to make AI a more integrated part of what I call the healthcare triad: the patient, the clinician, and the AI?

My role and approach

As the UX researcher for AMIE, I conducted qualitative interviews with patients and clinicians, partnering closely with Ava Homiar to drive this foundational qualitative research.

  • Patients: 20 interviews throughout the study
  • Clinicians: 10 interviews at the close of the study
  • AI supervisors: 5 interviews at the close of the study with the physicians who monitored AMIE's conversations

What we learned

Alongside the study's clinical results, what stood out was the human side of this technology.

  1. Empowering patients

    Talking with AMIE before their appointments helped patients organize their symptoms, frame their medical history, and get clear on their main concerns. This early conversation built patient confidence, so they could take a more active part in their face-to-face visits.

  2. The healthcare triad

    AMIE worked as a supportive third participant in the clinical relationship. Patients and providers both saw the AI as a collaborative tool that supported history taking, expanding the traditional patient and clinician pair into a balanced triad.

  3. The human connection

    With the AI handling the initial, time-consuming information gathering, clinicians saved time during consultations. That gave them more room to build rapport, respond to patients' emotional needs, and make decisions together with their patients.

Why it matters

This research is part of the rigor we're bringing to testing systems like AMIE. Alongside clinical measures of safety and accuracy, the qualitative work shows our dedication to a human-centered approach, bringing users into the process from design through testing.
